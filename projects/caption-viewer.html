Viewer

Captions are a compliance requirement, but whether a file actually carries them is invisible until something plays it. This puts the check in a browser: the reviewer watches the material with its captions rendered, then passes or fails it — and the decision is recorded against the file rather than remembered.

The problem

Captions have to be present and correct before material moves downstream, but a caption track gives no outward sign of itself. A file either carries one or it does not, and the only way to know is to play it with something that renders captions — which is not what a file listing, a checksum or a transfer report tells you.

So the check was happening late, informally, or not at all. When a caption problem was found after the fact, there was no record of who had looked at the file or what they saw.

The build

Material lands in a watch folder and appears in a review queue. The reviewer opens it, watches with captions rendered over the picture, and makes one decision. The file moves accordingly and the decision is written down.

  • Watch-folder ingest from existing storage paths, rather than asking anyone to upload material by hand
  • In-browser playback with captions rendered natively over the video
  • Pass or fail in one action, with reject reasons drawn from a fixed list so the outcome is consistent between reviewers
  • Approved files move to the delivery destination; rejected files go to a separate failed-file path, so the two never mix
  • Sign-off stamped into the accompanying metadata — who approved it, and when — and written to a separate reports path split by pass and fail
  • Read-only by design: the tool inspects and routes, and never edits or re-encodes the original

Captions hide in more places than you would expect

There is no single way a caption reaches a file, and a checker that only understands one of them will confidently report a file as uncaptioned when it is not. The awkward part of this build was coverage.

  • EIA-608 and 708 carried inside the video elementary stream
  • Muxed text tracks in their own stream
  • DVB and teletext subtitles arriving in transport streams
  • Sidecar subtitle files delivered alongside the media rather than inside it
  • Ancillary caption tracks in broadcast MXF, where the captions sit in a separate data track rather than in the video

The tool reports whether captions were found and whether they could be rendered, which matters as much as the pass or fail itself — "no captions present" and "captions present but unreadable" are different problems with different owners.

Decisions that shaped it

Caption rendering uses the browser's own subtitle support rather than a custom overlay renderer. A bespoke renderer would have allowed exact styling control, but it also means writing a caption renderer — and the thing being verified is whether captions exist and read correctly, not whether they are pixel-identical to a broadcast decoder.

Material arriving without its accompanying metadata is held rather than processed, because a file that passes review with nothing to stamp the approval into produces an unauditable result. An administrator can relax this when the workflow calls for it.

The outcome

Caption verification became an explicit gate with an owner and a record, instead of an assumption made somewhere upstream. Files that fail are separated at the point of review rather than discovered downstream, and every pass carries the name of the person who made the call.
