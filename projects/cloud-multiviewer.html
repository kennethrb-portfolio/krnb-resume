Monitoring

Cloud Playout Multiviewer
with Recorder

Cloud playout channels had no single place to watch them and no fast way to prove what went to air. This puts every channel on one browser tile wall and gives the operator a record button on each tile — turning a proof-of-airing request from an afternoon of retrieval into a few minutes of work.

The problem

Playout moved to the cloud, but monitoring did not follow. Channels could only be watched one browser tab at a time, which is no way to keep eyes on two dozen of them. There was no wall, no shared view, and nothing that behaved like the monitoring stack an operator already knows.

Proof of airing was the sharper problem. When a client or a regulator asked what actually went out, answering meant locating the material and pulling it together by hand — hours of work for a question that is usually about ninety seconds of airtime.

The build

A Node.js service ingests every channel over WebRTC and republishes it locally, so the browser wall and the recorder both read from one internal pipeline rather than each holding its own upstream connection. The wall itself is a plain browser page, reachable from any workstation on the network.

  • Tile wall with tabbed channel groups, plus a multi-select view for watching several groups stacked together
  • Click-to-solo audio in the MCR convention — all tiles muted, click one to listen, click again to drop it
  • Double-click any tile for fullscreen; right-click to force an immediate reconnect
  • One-click record per tile, written as fragmented MP4 so a crash mid-capture still leaves a playable file
  • Automatic retry when a feed drops mid-recording, and no automatic deletion of captures

Keeping the wall honest

A monitoring wall that shows a frozen picture is worse than one that shows nothing, because it reports good news that isn't true. Two independent watchdogs run against every tile.

  • A decoder check reads playback statistics per tile and reconnects when frames stop advancing
  • A content check samples each tile's brightness on a downscaled canvas, catching a picture stuck black while the decoder happily continues — a state no playback statistic can see
  • The black-frame timeout is set longer than the decoder timeout on purpose, so legitimate slates and bumpers don't trigger a reconnect
  • Recovery is silent and per-tile: one tile reconnects on its own without disturbing the other twenty-three

The outcome

Proof-of-airing turnaround dropped from hours to minutes. Monitoring that previously wasn't practical at all now runs on an unattended wall, and the operator who needs a capture takes it themselves in one click instead of raising a request and waiting.
